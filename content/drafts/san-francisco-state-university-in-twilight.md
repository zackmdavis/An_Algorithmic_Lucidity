I've been crashing Prof. Lai's "Advanced Topics" class this semester, on Fourier analysis plus enough functional analysis to get by (since the University can't afford to offer functional analysis as a regular class anymore). It's not obviously a good use of my time, but now that I've started, I have to see it through. I could try to tell a story about _impact_: I am leveling up my math chops, which will be useful for understanding natural abstractions or singular learning theory or mechanistic explanation, which will be useful for—but maybe it's less embarrassing to not try to sell a story. Call it luxury consumption during garbage time. I've lived a good life, in some ways if not others. I could choose to drown in oceans of regret over all the songs I never finished and stories I never wrote and math I never learned when I was young, in the before-time when there was still enough clock in the timeline for these things to matter—or I could choose to do something with the time I have now, which doesn't.

On Wednesday 16 September, the class had its first examination. (When I had dropped by office hours on Monday, Prof. Lai had said I could take it, but that he might not grade it.) I hadn't prepped enough and did poorly, blanking on two parts. I didn't hand in the paper, but GPT-6 Astra later gave it 58%.

After enjoying lunch and [dancing](/blog/2026/Oct/dance-dance/) at the [mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonestown_Galleria), I walked back to campus for the math department's occasional [seminar](https://sites.google.com/view/sfsuagc/fall-2026). On a whim, I had recently ordered a custom branded department tee (in Hebrew, with the thought of provoking a reaction from the other analysis professor, who is a big free-Palestine guy), which I was sure to wear for the occasion. (Ordering custom T-shirts online was already a thing in the before-time, but my modest [GIMP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GIMP) skills would not have yielded as attractive a result in as little effort.) Aida Abiad of the Eindhoven University of Technology was speaking on coding theory applications of spectral graph theory, a topic of nonzero interest to me. (In October 2009, I had ordered a copy of _An Introduction to the Theory of Graph Spectra_, which sat mostly untouched among my textbook collection for the intervening seventeen years.)

The talk was pretty accessible, as these things go. The independence number $\alpha$ of a graph is the maximum size of [a set of non-adjacent vertices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_set_(graph_theory)). The exact independence number of a graph is [hard to compute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hardness), but back in the 1970s, people had come up with some upper bounds in terms of the [eigenvalues](/blog/2012/Dec/eigencritters/) of the graph's [adjacency matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjacency_matrix). Abiad explained her work on extending such techniques to bound the _k_-independence number $\alpha_k$ (the maximum size of a set of vertices with graph distance greater than _k_ from each other). Once you have a bound in terms of powers of the eigenvalues, it's natural to ask, what about a polynomial?—and then, which polynomial is the best? Then there are applications: if you're constructing a code and a metric on your set of possible codewords is a graph distance, then the _k_-independence number of the graph tells you about the largest code you can construct whose codewords are greater than _k_ distance apart.

For the most part, it felt like that talk in that room could have taken place any time in the past seventeen or twenty-seven years. (Much further back than that, and it's harder to believe that SFSU would have had a digital projector rather than still being on [transparencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transparency_(projection)).) Abiad discussed bound improvements published in 2016, 2019, and 2022, but as far as I could tell as a non-specialist, there was no reason the same results couldn't have been reached in 2006 or 2009 or 2012 except that no one had gotten around to it yet. Besides the paper dates, the relative timelessness of the moment was only betrayed by a few odd remarks Abiad made, like, "Now probably the artificial intelligence gives you the graph invariants if you ask for it."

Afterwards, I took the 28 bus back to Daly City BART station. At the station, the bus stopped before pulling all the way up to the curb. Apparently a car was in the way: the bus driver honked a few times, then more frequently, then in one long, continuous honk. Why wasn't the car moving? "It's a Waymo!" one of the passengers said. "Fucking Waymo," said someone else. "Bring back human drivers!" said a man. The bus driver gave up and opened the doors, still short of the curb. Walking to the station among the throng, I saw that the offending Waymo had moved out of the way; it was unclear what had aroused it to motion.

On the train home, inspiration struck. Recently, one Levent Alpöge of Anthropic (and before that, I think someplace called "Harvert") had been in everyone's feed for Tweeting LLM-assisted solutions to famous open problems. I didn't have access to the frontier magic that Alpöge had, but I wondered if there was more low-hanging fruit accessible to the public models that hadn't been plucked yet just because there were few enough math people monitoring the AI situation that no one had spent the tokens yet. (My impression from hanging out at SFSU had been that everyone knew about LLMs as a way for students to cheat on homework—that's why Prof. Lai couldn't give take-home exams anymore—or for researchers to look up graph invariants, but that few were extrapolating.)

When I got home, I tasked Claude Opus 5 in Claude Code with going through the papers of Prof. Emily Clader (who had let me [crash her "Topics" class on algebraic topology this time last year](/docs/algebraic_topology-01.pdf)) to look for "open problems that might be interesting to her that might be scoopable with an LLM (so, like, stuff where you can compute a checkable counterexample, which would have been a master's student project)". I said that scooping the result would "probably demand expensive Fable tokens, although Opus is probably fine" for the literature search.

What transpired next happened quickly enough that I can only reconstruct it from the Claude Code session log—with the help of [annotations from](/docs/session_2026-09-16_proof-annotated.html) Claude Opus 5.5 (released 22 September), since the raw JSONL is so unpleasant to read.

Opus 5 had downloaded the LaTeX sources of Clader's papers from the ArXiv and grepped for phrases like "open problem", "conjecture" and "future work." "Found a promising one already", it reported after 84 seconds: [Clader, Damiolini, Li, and Ramadas 2023 (revised 2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.05463v3) "conjectures that the Betti numbers of $\overline{\mathcal{L}}^r_n$ are log-concave."

It continued reading abstracts and grepping for open-problem prompts in a few other papers for another 48 seconds, then decided, "I'll focus on the log-concavity conjecture since it's explicit, computable, and only lightly checked in the original table." The conjecture in the paper had been preceded by a table of the aforementioned Betti numbers for several 2 ≤ _r_ ≤ 4 and 3 ≤ _n_ ≤ 7. "First I'll reproduce their table before searching for any later resolutions." (These words appear in a `"type":"thinking"` entry in the log, presumably written by a summarizer model based on Opus 5's original chain of thought, which isn't exposed to users.)

Four seconds later, it had written a Python script to reproduce the table, which succeeded. It took another eight seconds to write another Python script to check log-concavity up to _r_ ≤ 15 and _n_ ≤ 40, and "real-rootedness, which implies it" up to _r_ ≤ 15 and _n_ ≤ 25. That program took half a minute to run. "No counterexamples turned up", said the thinking summary, "every case is log-concave and real-rooted, so real-rootedness likely implies log-concavity here. I'll check if this result already exists before attempting a proof."

In parallel with web search and Semantic Scholar citations failing to turn up an existing proof, it did a web search for `Athanasiadis binomial Eulerian polynomials colored permutations real-rooted`. "I see this differs from" the _r_ = 2 case in Clader _et al._, said the thinking summary on reading [Athanasiadis's paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.00434), "but the same technique applies. I'll look for a recurrence satisfied by f_n to prove real-rootedness via interlacing." It took another seven seconds to write a Python script to search for a recurrence expressing the generating function for the Betti numbers of $\overline{\mathcal{L}}^r_{n+1}$ (a polynomial, since there are only finitely many nonzero Betti numbers) in terms of the generating polynomials for $\overline{\mathcal{L}}^r_n$, $\overline{\mathcal{L}}^r_{n-1}$, and their derivatives: given the actual Betti numbers and a guess about what form of recurrence might generate them, finding the coëfficients of the recurrence (if the guess was right) becomes a routine linear algebra computation.

The script crashed. Opus 5 fixed the bug, but the script didn't turn up anything. "Rather than widen the search blindly, I'll read Haglund–Zhang's 'Real-rootedness of variations of Eulerian polynomials'", reported Opus 5, referring to another paper that had turned up in the previous web search. "Their methods may apply directly." Its first attempt to locate the theorem statements in the LaTeX of the [Haglund and Zhang paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.01278) failed, both because it was grepping for "theorem" (and "lemma", "proposition", "conjecture", _&c._) whereas the paper used an abbreviation "thm" defined in the preamble, and because the file contained an invalid UTF-8 byte sequence, causing it to be interpreted as a binary file and ignored by Claude Code's version of `grep`. After converting the file encoding and adjusting the query, Opus 5 was able to read from the paper and write another Python script to check whether the Betti-number-generating-polynomial matched anything from the Haglund and Zhang paper: no dice.

Opus 5 thought for 32 seconds. It spent 7 seconds writing another Python script to find a recurrence. The script took 13 seconds to run—and succeeded. "Next I'll derive this recurrence symbolically from the generating function to confirm it rigorously rather than relying on the fit," said the thinking summary. It wrote two more scripts and did some more web research if the conjecture had been resolved elsewhere, then spent 20 seconds writing up a Markdown file of the result and another few seconds on an edit.

"I think we scooped something," it reported, nine minutes and twenty seconds after I had told it to start working. ("We"!)

"I'll try to read that, but ... that took less than ten minutes of sidereal time!" I typed.
