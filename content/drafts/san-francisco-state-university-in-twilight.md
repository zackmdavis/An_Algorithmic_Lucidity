I've been crashing Prof. Lai's "Advanced Topics" class this semester, on Fourier analysis plus enough functional analysis to get by (since the University can't afford to offer functional analysis as a regular class anymore). It's not obviously a good use of my time, but now that I've started, I have to see it through. I could try to tell a story about _impact_: I am leveling up my math chops, which will be useful for understanding natural abstractions or singular learning theory or mechanistic explanation, which will be useful for—but maybe it's less embarrassing to not try to sell a story. Call it luxury consumption during garbage time. I've lived a good life, in some ways if not others. I could choose to drown in oceans of regret over all the songs I never finished and stories I never wrote and math I never learned when I was young, in the before-time when there was still enough clock in the timeline for these things to matter—or I could choose to do something with the time I have now, which doesn't.

Only it's not quite the same as it was in the before-time. Part of the glamor of learning math in the before-time lay in the irreplaceability of expensive human capital investment. Textbooks contained information, but information could only become living knowledge through uncounted hours of laborious struggle, whose pain could only be tolerated on the promise that it expanded the reach of one's intellect and kept the flame of civilization alive.

It's not completely different now. What's still true is that information can only become _human_ knowledge through uncounted hours of laborious struggle. And as far as the production of human knowledge goes, the present end-time should be much superior to anything that came before. Anything you would have done in the before-time, you can still do. Nothing has been taken away. It's just that now you have the option of chatting with an indefatigably patient tutor who knows everything, is always online, and works for $20 a month.

It should be much superior. Some of the struggle to compile information into human knowledge is intrinsic to the endeavor (one aspires to learn to solve problems, not merely be told how to solve problems), but too much of the struggles of the before-time were unnecessary. Think of all the moments when a minimal well-timed hint from a tutor could have converted the deathly suffering of being _stuck_ to the life-affirming suffering of productive struggle, but no tutor was available until scheduled office hours. Having the tutor live in your phone should amount to a fantastic efficiency gain.

After enjoying lunch and [dancing](https://zackmdavis.net/blog/2026/Oct/dance-dance/) at the [mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonestown_Galleria), I walked back to campus for the math department's semi-weekly [seminar](https://sites.google.com/view/sfsuagc/fall-2026). I had made sure to wear my custom branded department tee for the occasion. Ordering custom T-shirts online was already a thing in the before-time, but my graphic design skills then wouldn't have been up to the task. Aida Abiad of the Eindhoven University of Technology was speaking on coding theory applications of spectral graph theory, a topic of nonzero interest to me. (In October 2009, I had ordered _An Introduction to the Theory of Graph Spectra_, which had sat mostly untouched among my textbook collection for the intervening seventeen years.)

Afterwards, I took the 28 bus back to Daly City BART station. At the station, the bus stopped before pulling all the way up to the curb. Apparently a car was in the way: the bus driver honked a few times, then more frequently, then in one long, continuous honk. Why wasn't the car moving? "It's a Waymo!" one of the passengers said. "Fucking Waymo," said someone else. "Bring back human drivers!" said a man. The bus driver gave up and opened the doors, still short of the curb.
