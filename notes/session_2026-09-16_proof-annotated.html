The bridge from what you know

These spaces are complex manifolds. $\overline{\mathcal{L}}^r_n$ has complex dimension $n$, so it's a real $2n$-dimensional manifold. They also have a special property: all homology lives in even degrees and has no torsion, like $\mathbb{CP}^n$. So the only interesting numbers are $b_0, b_2, \dots, b_{2n}$, and the "Betti polynomial" is $$P(t) = \sum_i b_{2i}\,t^i.$$ The paper's $\dim A^i$ is just $b_{2i}$ for these spaces, so you can ignore Chow groups entirely.

Almost everything in the proof's starting point can be checked with tools you already have:

  1. $\mathbb{CP}^1 = S^2$: $P = 1+t$. $\mathbb{CP}^2$: $P = 1+t+t^2$, from cellular homology with one cell in each even dimension.
  2. Products multiply (Künneth, since there's no torsion): $\mathbb{CP}^1 \times \mathbb{CP}^1$ has $P = (1+t)^2$.
  3. Blowing up a point in a complex surface adds $t$. The point gets replaced by a $\mathbb{CP}^1$, so the Euler characteristic goes up by $\chi(S^2) - 1 = 1$, and the new class sits in $H_2$.
  4. The general rule (the blow-up formula, which you can take on faith): blowing up a submanifold $Z$ of complex codimension $c$ adds $P_Z(t)\,(t + t^2 + \dots + t^{c-1})$.

The key example: $n=2$. Take $\mathbb{CP}^1 \times \mathbb{CP}^1$, mark $r$ special points on each factor, and blow up the $r^2$ points where they cross. You get $$P_{r,2}(t) = (1+t)^2 + r^2 t = 1 + (r^2+2)t + t^2.$$ That's exactly what the paper's formula gives.

The general Li Li formula is this same bookkeeping done in all dimensions. Each factor $(t + \dots + t^{j-1})$ in it is item 4 applied to one blow-up, and each $(1+t)^{n-|\mathbf{j}|}$ is the Betti polynomial of the smaller product being blown up. So the scary formula is "start with $(\mathbb{CP}^1)^n$ and track how blow-ups change the Betti numbers."

Exercise: do $n=3$ by hand. First blow up the $r^3$ special points (codimension 3, each adds $t+t^2$). Then blow up the $3r^2$ special lines (each $\mathbb{CP}^1$ with codimension 2, each adds $(1+t)\,t$). Check that $r=2$ gives $1+23t+23t^2+t^3$.

$\mathbb{CP}^n$

$\mathbb{CP}^n$ is complex projective $n$-space: the set of lines through the origin in $\mathbb{C}^{n+1}$. A point is written $[z_0 : \dots : z_n]$, not all zero, where scaling by any nonzero complex number gives the same point.

  • $\mathbb{CP}^1$ is the Riemann sphere: $\mathbb{C}$ plus a point at infinity, topologically $S^2$. Its Betti numbers are $b_0 = b_2 = 1$.
  • $\mathbb{CP}^2$ is the complex projective plane. It's a real 4-manifold with one cell each in dimensions 0, 2, and 4, so $b_0=b_2=b_4=1$.

The construction for $n=2$

Start with $\mathbb{CP}^1 \times \mathbb{CP}^1 = S^2 \times S^2$, with Betti numbers $1, 2, 1$ by Künneth.

Mark $r$ special points on each factor. Pick $r$ points on the sphere, for instance the $r$-th roots of unity $1, \zeta, \dots, \zeta^{r-1}$.

The special loci are the spheres you get by fixing one coordinate at a special point:

  • $\{p\} \times S^2$ for each special $p$: $r$ "vertical" spheres;
  • $S^2 \times \{q\}$ for each special $q$: $r$ "horizontal" spheres.

Each vertical sphere meets each horizontal sphere in exactly one point, $(p, q)$. That's $r^2$ crossing points.

Blowing up a point replaces that point with the set of all complex directions through it. In a complex surface those directions form a $\mathbb{CP}^1$, a little 2-sphere called the exceptional sphere.

  • Real analogy: blowing up the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$ replaces it with the circle of real directions, $\mathbb{RP}^1$. The result is a Möbius band.
  • Why do it: the vertical and horizontal spheres crossed at $(p,q)$ in different directions. After the blow-up they pass through different points of the exceptional sphere, so they no longer meet. Blowing up untangles crossings.

The topological fact you can check with tools from Clader's course: blowing up a point in a complex surface $X$ is the connected sum $X \mathbin{\#} \overline{\mathbb{CP}^2}$, i.e. $\mathbb{CP}^2$ with reversed orientation. Mayer–Vietoris shows this adds exactly 1 to $b_2$. So $$\overline{\mathcal{L}}^r_2 = (S^2 \times S^2) \,\#\, r^2\ \text{copies of}\ \overline{\mathbb{CP}^2}, \qquad \text{Betti numbers } 1,\ r^2+2,\ 1.$$ That matches $P_{r,2}(t) = 1 + (r^2+2)t + t^2$ in the proof.

For larger $n$ the recipe is the same in $(\mathbb{CP}^1)^n$. The special loci are the places where some coordinates sit at special points, and you blow up all their crossings, smallest first. The proof's big sum formula is the bookkeeping for how each blow-up changes the Betti numbers.

What a matroid is. A matroid abstracts the notion of "independence." You have a finite set $E$ and a rule saying which subsets are independent, satisfying a few axioms. The key one is exchange: if $I$ and $J$ are independent and $|I| < |J|$, you can add some element of $J$ to $I$ and keep it independent. Two standard examples:

  • Vectors: $E$ is a finite set of vectors, and a subset is independent if it's linearly independent.
  • Graphs: $E$ is the edges of a graph, and a subset is independent if it contains no cycle, i.e. it's a forest.

Why log-concavity is in the air. A matroid has a characteristic polynomial, a generalization of a graph's chromatic polynomial. The Heron–Rota–Welsh conjecture said its coefficients are log-concave. It was open for about 50 years until Adiprasito–Huh–Katz proved it in 2018. They built a Chow ring for every matroid, even ones that don't come from vectors, and showed it behaves like the cohomology of a smooth projective variety. What it means here. You start with a space containing some special subspaces that cross badly, like the vertical and horizontal spheres meeting at points in the $n=2$ picture. You blow up the crossings in a prescribed order, smallest first. The result is wonderful in a precise sense:
